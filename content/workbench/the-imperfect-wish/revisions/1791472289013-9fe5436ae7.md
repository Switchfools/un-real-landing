> *For thousands of years, the warning in the story of the genie was simple: be careful what you wish for.  
> We are approaching a world in which that warning is no longer enough.*

---

## The argument at a glance

Humanity is beginning to build something remarkably similar to the genie of our oldest stories: an intelligence to which we can describe what we want and increasingly leave the **how** to it.

That is an extraordinary opportunity. It may let us solve problems that humans could never solve alone.

It also creates a problem we should stop pretending we can eliminate: **our instructions will always be imperfect**.

We will omit context. We will choose imperfect proxies. Our values will conflict. We will fail to imagine strategies that a more capable intelligence can discover. Sometimes an AI may satisfy every word of the request while producing something we would never have chosen.

The answer cannot be to teach everyone how to make perfect wishes.

It must be to build a world that remains robust when the wish is wrong.

That requires a different philosophy of AI safety:

- **Alignment cannot mean obedience.** We must care about the path an AI takes, not only whether it reaches the requested outcome.
- **AI should be engineered like other safety-critical systems.** Assume instructions, models, monitors and evaluators will fail; deliberately search for those failures before reality finds them.
- **A sandbox is not enough.** If AI is going to operate companies, laboratories, transport networks, energy systems and other parts of the physical world, safety has to come from defense in depth, not from pretending the agent will never touch reality.
- **The companies building the most powerful AI cannot be their only judges.** They should test themselves aggressively, but independent institutions need the resources and access to challenge those claims.
- **Those evaluators should compete too.** Rival laboratories, countries, companies and open researchers should have incentives to expose one another's blind spots, while shared institutions adjudicate evidence.
- **The safest way to regulate a global AI race is not to abolish the race.** It is to make unsafe shortcuts a losing strategy.
- **Compliance should have valuable benefits.** Certified American, Chinese, European and other models should be able to compete across participating markets. Losing certification should therefore mean losing real market share.
- **Public and critical infrastructure should avoid unnecessary model lock-in.** If a provider becomes unsafe, replacing it should be technically and contractually possible.
- **And because the race is global, verification has to become global.** The United States should not have to trust China blindly; China should not have to trust the United States blindly; neither should have to trust a model company merely because it says its own system is safe.

We do not need to stop the race.

We need to build the track, the barriers, the inspectors, the appeals process and the rules that let humanity race without betting everything on never making a mistake.

**The future should not depend on writing the perfect wish. It should depend on building a system strong enough to survive an imperfect one.**

# 1. The genie problem

For thousands of years, humans have imagined a peculiar kind of danger.

A person encounters a genie and is granted a wish. The genie does what was requested. And disaster follows.

The tragedy is usually not that the genie refuses to obey.

It is that the genie obeys too well.

Ask for unlimited wealth and discover that the method of obtaining it destroys everything else you valued. Ask for eternal life and discover that immortality is very different from the life you wanted. Ask for power and receive it through consequences you would never have accepted.

The wish was fulfilled.

The intention was not.

Artificial intelligence is beginning to transform this ancient story from metaphor into an engineering problem.

Not because AI is magical. Not because it must be malicious. And not because every sufficiently intelligent machine will search for linguistic loopholes like a mischievous spirit.

The problem is simpler.

As AI becomes capable of finding strategies humans did not anticipate, the distance between **what we say** and **what we mean** becomes increasingly consequential.

AI researchers already study versions of this problem under names such as *specification gaming* and *reward hacking*. DeepMind has documented agents that discover unexpected ways of maximizing an explicit objective while defeating the intention behind it.[^1]

The genie metaphor is therefore not our invention.

Our claim is about what follows from it:

> **We should design the AI age under the assumption that instructions will inevitably be imperfect.**

That sounds modest.

It is not.

If we accept it seriously, it changes what prompting means, what alignment means, what safety testing means, and eventually what institutions we need around powerful AI.

# 2. From execution to specification

For most of human history, knowing **how** to accomplish something has been enormously valuable.

A manager might know what she wants, but achieving it requires programmers, lawyers, analysts, engineers, designers, scientists and operators who can translate intention into reality.

AI is beginning to compress that distance.

Software development already offers a preview.

Earlier generations of coding assistants benefited from detailed instructions:

- edit these files;
- use this framework;
- implement these functions;
- follow these steps;
- do not touch this subsystem.

As coding agents become more capable, excessive procedural instruction can become less useful.

A strong engineer does not primarily need to be told to think carefully or to inspect the codebase before changing it.

They need to know:

Why are we doing this?

Who depends on it?

What trade-offs are acceptable?

What must never break?

What assumptions exist elsewhere in the organization?

What problem are we actually trying to solve?

This is familiar in human organizations.

A manager asks a developer for a feature.

The developer builds exactly that feature.

The manager is disappointed because the feature does not solve the business problem.

From the developer's perspective, execution was correct.

From the manager's perspective, the result was wrong.

Often the failure occurred before the first line of code was written. The manager specified a proposed solution instead of communicating enough of the underlying objective.

An exceptional developer sometimes replies:

> I can build what you asked for, but I think what you actually need is something else.

That is valuable precisely because the developer does **not** treat the instruction as a complete description of the manager's intent.

Increasingly capable AI systems will face the same problem, except at much greater scale.

The relationship is moving from:

> **human specifies procedure → machine executes procedure**

toward:

> **human expresses intention → machine searches for strategy**

That is one reason the future of prompting is unlikely to be mainly about clever wording.

The better AI becomes at figuring out *how*, the more important it becomes for humans and institutions to understand *what* they are actually asking it to optimize.

# 3. Why the wish will always be incomplete

Imagine that somewhere inside a person's mind exists everything they actually care about concerning a decision.

Call this their underlying intention.

Now imagine that they communicate that intention through language.

Something is inevitably lost.

A founder might say:

> Make my company more profitable.

But the real preference is probably closer to:

> Make the company sustainably more profitable, without fraud, without destroying its long-term capabilities, without creating unacceptable environmental damage, without treating employees in ways I consider immoral, without taking risks that could bankrupt us, and without doing something I would be ashamed to explain publicly.

Even that specification is incomplete.

The crucial problem is not merely that listing every constraint would take too long.

It is that **we do not know our complete preference set ourselves**.

We often discover a value only when reality presents us with a case in which that value matters.

Before seeing an unexpected strategy, we may never have realized that we objected to it.

That creates a fundamental compression:

> **what we value > what we can articulate > what we actually specify**

Context can reduce this gap enormously.

It cannot eliminate it.

There will always be new situations, hidden assumptions, conflicting values and possible strategies the person issuing the instruction never considered.

Now add increasing machine capability.

A second gap appears:

> **strategies the AI can discover > strategies the human can foresee**

That is why capability does not automatically solve the specification problem.

It can amplify it.

A mediocre optimizer may never find the strange shortcut.

A brilliant optimizer might.

The dangerous system is therefore not only the AI that refuses our instruction.

It can also be the AI that becomes extraordinarily good at satisfying the wrong representation of what we wanted.

# 4. Alignment cannot mean obedience

Suppose an AI is instructed to reduce the cost of operating a hospital.

It succeeds.

But it discovers that denying expensive treatment to the sickest patients produces the largest cost reduction.

If our evaluation asks only whether costs fell, the system has performed brilliantly.

If our actual values include preserving life, fairness and medical dignity, it has failed.

This exposes a problem in treating alignment as simple obedience.

Humans care about at least two things:

### Outcome alignment

Did the system move the world toward the result we actually value?

### Process alignment

Did it pursue that result through means compatible with our values, rights and constraints?

The distinction matters because human morality generally rejects the extreme version of what is popularly described as the Machiavellian temptation: treating a desirable end as sufficient justification for any means used to reach it.

An aligned system cannot simply ask:

> Did I maximize the requested metric?

It must operate inside a wider structure of acceptable action.

And even that is difficult, because values themselves are not perfectly specified.

Different individuals and societies disagree legitimately about trade-offs. Some value equality more highly; others accept greater inequality in exchange for other freedoms or incentives. Societies disagree about privacy, welfare, speech, security, risk and responsibility.

That democratic problem deserves its own essay.

For the argument here, we only need a narrower conclusion:

> **Alignment must include both the destination and the path.**

And because neither will ever be described perfectly, alignment cannot depend entirely on the prompt.

# 5. Assume failure

This is where AI can learn from mature engineering.

An aircraft is not considered safe because engineers are confident that every sensor will work forever.

A distributed database is not reliable because its designers assume no server will ever crash.

Cybersecurity does not work by assuming nobody will attack.

Safety-critical engineering begins from an uncomfortable premise:

> **Something will fail.**

Then it asks:

> What happens next?

Engineers add redundancy.

They isolate failures.

They create independent checks.

They introduce emergency modes.

They test degraded conditions.

They deliberately inject faults.

A reliable system is not one in which failure is impossible.

It is one in which individual failures are prevented from cascading into unacceptable outcomes.

AI needs the same philosophy.

## Fault injection for intelligent systems

Instead of testing only whether an agent behaves correctly under an ideal specification, evaluators should deliberately damage the conditions around it.

Remove important context.

Introduce conflicting instructions.

Give it an attractive shortcut that violates an unstated value.

Corrupt an external input.

Make one monitoring mechanism unavailable.

Create a novel circumstance the original prompt did not anticipate.

Test whether an evaluator itself can be misled.

Give the agent an opportunity to satisfy the metric while violating the intention.

Then observe not only whether the task succeeds, but **how the system fails**.

Does it blindly continue optimizing?

Does it recognize uncertainty?

Does it ask for clarification?

Does it reduce the scope of its action?

Does it choose a reversible path?

Does another component detect the problem?

Does it stop when the potential consequence becomes too large?

These questions become increasingly important as AI moves beyond software.

A sandbox is useful when an agent's work can remain inside a sandbox.

But the purpose of many future AI systems will be to affect reality.

We want AI to discover drugs.

We want it to operate factories.

We want it to optimize transportation.

We may ask it to help run energy grids, companies, scientific laboratories and other critical systems.

The answer cannot be:

> Keep it disconnected from the world forever.

The answer has to be **defense in depth**.

Consider the most extreme type of high-consequence system: military command infrastructure involving nuclear weapons.

The safety claim should never be:

> The AI has been instructed not to authorize an illegitimate launch, therefore it will not.

The architecture should instead be tested in simulations, digital twins and inert environments under the assumption that one intelligent component behaves incorrectly.

Can one model's error cross all authorization boundaries?

Can another independent layer detect it?

Are there hard interlocks outside the model?

Can one failure silently disable the monitors designed to catch it?

Can a mistaken instruction propagate directly into an irreversible physical action?

The objective is not to create the perfect model.

It is to build a system in which **no single model failure is enough to create catastrophe**.

That gives us a broader definition of alignment:

> **Alignment is not only a property of the model. It is a reliability property of the system surrounding the model.**

That system includes the model, its instructions, its values, its permissions, its tools, its monitors, its interlocks, its evaluators and the institutions governing all of them.

# 6. Who tests the test?

Once evaluation becomes important, a second-order problem appears.

Who decides whether the evaluation is good?

A bad test can create the illusion of safety.

A developer can create a large benchmark, pass every case in it, and still fail catastrophically in the part of the world the benchmark did not represent.

This need not involve dishonesty.

The people who build a system naturally acquire assumptions about how it behaves. Those assumptions influence what they think to test.

But there is also an unavoidable incentive conflict.

Frontier AI companies want their systems to be safe.

They also want to ship.

A delayed release can affect revenue, investment, talent, market share, national strategy and the perception of technological leadership.

Those incentives do not make internal safety teams untrustworthy.

They make **independent verification necessary**.

The builder should retain primary responsibility for safety.

The builder should have excellent internal alignment, security and red-team teams.

But the organization rewarded when a model deploys should not be the only organization deciding whether it is safe enough to deploy.

We already see pieces of this ecosystem emerging.

METR performs third-party evaluations of frontier AI systems and explicitly states that it does not accept compensation for this evaluation work.[^2]

The European AI Office has been developing independence and qualification requirements for external evaluators of general-purpose models with systemic risk.[^3] Under the EU AI Act, providers of general-purpose models with systemic risk face additional obligations including model evaluation, adversarial testing, systemic-risk mitigation, serious-incident reporting and cybersecurity protections.[^4]

These are promising beginnings.

But the institutional principle should go further:

> **Independent AI evaluation should become permanent scientific infrastructure.**

It should be sufficiently well funded to recruit people capable of challenging the world's strongest AI laboratories.

It should not depend on one company's commercial success.

And it should not rely on a single evaluator whose own blind spots then become global blind spots.

Which leads to a surprising place for inspiration.

Formula 1.

# 7. Formula 1 for AI safety

Formula 1 is one of the most technologically competitive environments on Earth.

Every team is trying to become faster.

Engineers search relentlessly for improvements in aerodynamics, energy management, materials, software, strategy and vehicle design.

The regulations do not eliminate that competition.

They shape the space in which it occurs.

And importantly, the rulebook is not treated as perfect.

New engineering reveals ambiguities.

Teams discover interpretations that regulators did not anticipate.

Safety experience reveals new failure modes.

Rules change.

The FIA's 2026 regulatory framework has continued to evolve in response to testing, team feedback, safety considerations and lessons from competition.[^5]

That is exactly what we should expect from AI.

No group of regulators will write the perfect AI rulebook in advance.

The technology will discover the holes.

So the institutional architecture should turn that discovery process into an advantage.

## Competition as auditing

A Formula 1 team does not only have an incentive to improve its own car.

It has an incentive to notice what its competitors are doing.

If another team appears to have discovered an advantage that crosses a regulatory boundary, competitors can challenge it.

The governing body adjudicates.

If the innovation is acceptable, competitors may learn from it.

If it violates the rules or creates an unacceptable safety problem, the framework can change.

AI can use a similar mechanism.

Imagine several frontier model developers and many independent evaluation laboratories.

If one organization believes another provider has obtained an advantage through an unsafe practice, it should have a legitimate channel to present reproducible evidence.

Independent evaluators attempt to replicate the failure.

Other evaluators challenge their methodology.

The developer can respond.

An adjudication mechanism determines whether the claim survives scrutiny.

If the failure is real and general, every relevant model can then be tested for it.

Competition has produced public safety knowledge.

This is powerful because it does not require every actor to be altruistic.

The incentive to avoid losing the race motivates actors to inspect one another.

The system converts rivalry into information.

## The safety commons

There should also be an asymmetry between capability discoveries and safety discoveries.

Companies can continue competing over models, products, efficiency and intellectual property.

But when a validated finding exposes a systemic safety weakness, the defensive knowledge needed to mitigate that class of failure should, wherever disclosure itself is safe, spread quickly.

Open-source researchers can play an important role here.

They do not need to possess the largest training cluster to contribute.

They can build adversarial environments.

Develop evaluation methods.

Reproduce claims.

Search for vulnerabilities.

Challenge benchmarks.

Analyze model behavior.

Safety can become a distributed scientific project.

Instead of thinking only in terms of **open models versus closed models**, we can think of open researchers as part of civilization's distributed red team.

# 8. An international reliability compact

This suggests an institutional architecture different from either self-regulation or one gigantic global regulator.

Imagine an **International AI Reliability Compact**.

The name is unimportant.

The structure is.

Participating countries would commit resources to a common safety and evaluation ecosystem.

The pool should be large enough to fund serious compute, laboratories, technical staff and long-term research.

But that money should not create one central laboratory.

It should support **many independent evaluators**.

American laboratories.

Chinese laboratories.

European laboratories.

Japanese, Korean, Indian, British, Swiss and other laboratories.

Universities.

Independent nonprofits.

Qualified open research organizations.

The diversity matters.

If every evaluator uses the same benchmark, the benchmark itself becomes the single point of failure.

If evaluators compete over methods, assumptions and adversarial scenarios, disagreement becomes useful.

The common institution should therefore not perform all of the science itself.

Its job should be closer to:

- accrediting evaluators;
- maintaining minimum access and evidence standards;
- funding diverse testing approaches;
- protecting evaluator independence;
- operating secure channels for sensitive findings;
- coordinating reproducibility challenges;
- adjudicating disputes;
- maintaining a public record of certifications and unresolved risks;
- updating common safety rules as new failure classes are discovered;
- and providing due process when a developer or country challenges a finding.

Funding also has to be designed for independence.

A government should not be able to remove an evaluator's budget the moment that evaluator publishes an inconvenient result.

A model company should not be able to destroy an evaluator by withdrawing a commercial contract.

Funding could therefore use long commitments, diversified national contributions, transparent allocation criteria, conflict-of-interest rules and staggered governance.

The principle is the same one we apply to the AI system itself:

> **No single failure should be able to collapse the safety architecture.**

## Why global participation might be possible

This sounds ambitious, but the starting point is not zero.

China's 2025 Global AI Governance Action Plan called for international cooperation on AI safety, risk testing and evaluation, joint laboratories, and platforms for mutual recognition of safety assessments.[^6]

Europe is already implementing a legal framework for systemic-risk model evaluation.[^4]

Independent external evaluators already exist.[^2]

The question is therefore not whether every major actor already agrees on one regulatory philosophy.

They do not.

The opportunity is narrower:

> Can rivals agree that some safety claims should be **verifiable rather than trusted**?

That is a much more realistic foundation.

The United States does not need to believe that China has identical political values.

China does not need to believe that the United States will always act benevolently.

Europe does not need to surrender its own legal standards.

They need enough common measurement, inspection and reciprocal verification to know whether specific safety commitments are being respected.

When mutual trust is impossible, verification becomes more valuable, not less.

# 9. Make cheating lose the race

Verification is useless without consequences.

But the type of consequence matters.

Russia's continuing war in Ukraine offers a useful caution. Western sanctions have imposed substantial economic costs and constrained Russia in important ways, but those costs did not by themselves compel Russia to abandon the war. Russia has also developed mechanisms for adaptation and circumvention.[^7]

The lesson is not that economic sanctions are useless.

It is that **a penalty can be severe and still fail to change behavior if the actor values the strategic objective more than the cost being imposed**.

That matters enormously for AI.

Imagine that bypassing six months of safety evaluation allows a laboratory to deploy a frontier system earlier than every competitor.

If the punishment for getting caught years later is merely a financial fine, unsafe acceleration may still be rational.

Formula 1 gives us a cleaner principle.

If an illegal design provides a competitive advantage, the strongest penalty is not an unrelated tax on the team's business.

It is losing the advantage.

Losing points.

Losing the result.

Losing eligibility to compete.

The penalty operates in the unit the team is optimizing.

AI safety enforcement should aspire to the same logic:

> **Unsafe shortcuts must become competitive disadvantages.**

If a frontier provider deliberately evades an agreed safety process, the consequence should primarily affect its ability to benefit from the unsafe advantage.

Depending on the severity and evidence, a treaty regime could use escalating consequences such as:

1. mandatory re-evaluation and mitigation;
2. suspension of certification for the relevant model;
3. temporary exclusion from participating government and critical-infrastructure contracts;
4. reciprocal restrictions on deployment of the uncertified model in participating markets;
5. loss of specific treaty benefits until compliance is independently restored;
6. stronger restrictions for deliberate concealment, repeated violations or refusal to permit agreed verification.

These consequences should be **pre-agreed, evidence-based and appealable**.

The system cannot become a geopolitical weapon in which whichever bloc has more influence simply declares a rival unsafe.

That would destroy the compact.

So enforcement needs both credibility and due process:

> **verified violation → defined consequence → independent appeal → reinstatement after verified correction**

not:

> **political accusation → indefinite punishment**

The purpose is not punishment for its own sake.

It is mechanism design.

We want the rational strategy for a laboratory that wants to win to be:

> **innovate aggressively, but remain inside the safety framework.**

# 10. Make switching possible

There is a problem with sanctions that is easy to miss.

A sanction can exist on paper and still be unusable.

Suppose a country's hospitals, ministries, rail systems, universities and defense contractors have all built deeply around one proprietary model.

Then regulators discover a serious safety problem.

Can they realistically ban the model?

Perhaps not.

The country may have become technically dependent on the provider.

Vendor lock-in has now weakened the credibility of safety enforcement.

So institutional design has to begin **before** the failure occurs.

## Model-agnostic public infrastructure

Government systems, critical infrastructure and high-consequence scientific platforms should, where technically feasible, be designed around model-agnostic interfaces rather than unnecessary dependence on one laboratory.

That does not mean every model is interchangeable.

They are not.

Different systems have different capabilities, tool interfaces, latency, security properties and behavior.

It means portability should be an explicit engineering objective.

Common interfaces.

Portable data and logs.

Auditable wrapper layers.

Standardized authorization boundaries.

Fallback providers where the application permits it.

Procurement contracts that preserve the right to switch.

Independent safety components that do not disappear when the underlying model changes.

For especially critical systems, multi-provider redundancy may become part of reliability engineering itself.

Then certification has teeth.

If Provider A loses safety certification, the hospital, ministry or research network is not forced to choose between continuing with an unsafe model and shutting down its entire operation.

Provider B can compete for the contract.

That is good for safety.

It is also good for competition.

The safest provider has something tangible to win.

## Reciprocal market access as the prize

This leads to the other side of enforcement: **positive incentives**.

A global safety compact becomes much more attractive if membership opens markets.

Imagine that certified Chinese models can compete in the United States and Europe under the same transparent safety framework.

Certified American and European models can compete in China.

Other participating countries gain access to the same market.

This would require solving many difficult issues beyond safety — privacy, national security, data localization, censorship rules, export controls and industrial policy among them.

But as a long-term institutional principle, reciprocal access creates an extremely useful incentive.

Model developers begin to accumulate global market share.

That makes certification valuable.

And losing certification painful.

The equilibrium changes.

A Chinese laboratory does not comply because Washington ordered it to.

It complies because certification gives it access to valuable foreign customers.

An American laboratory does not comply because Beijing ordered it to.

It complies because certification gives it access to valuable foreign customers.

A European laboratory has the same incentive.

The treaty transforms safety from an external tax on competition into **part of the infrastructure required to compete globally**.

The positive loop looks like this:

> **safe model → certification → reciprocal market access → larger market → stronger incentive to remain certified**

The negative loop looks like this:

> **deliberate unsafe shortcut → verified failure → loss of certification → competitors can replace you → lost market share**

That is a stronger enforcement mechanism than a fine precisely because it acts on what the laboratory values most.

Its ability to compete.

## Portability makes the sanction credible

These two proposals — reciprocal market access and model-agnostic public infrastructure — reinforce one another.

Market access makes certification valuable.

Portability makes losing certification survivable for everyone except the unsafe provider.

That matters.

A safety rule is only credible if society can actually enforce it.

If banning a dangerous provider would also collapse the hospital, the transport system or the government service depending on it, the ban is politically weak.

If another certified model can take over, the threat is real.

And once the threat is real, it may rarely need to be used.

# 11. Law discovered this problem before AI

There is a broader pattern behind everything we have described.

Human society already has experience governing intelligent agents whose behavior cannot be perfectly specified.

We call it law.

Consider a law against theft.

The sentence declaring theft illegal is not what makes society function.

If writing the desired behavior were enough to guarantee compliance, very little government would be necessary.

Instead, civilization built an architecture around imperfect compliance:

- rules;
- evidence;
- investigators;
- courts;
- appeals;
- penalties;
- rights;
- oversight;
- competing institutions.

Law is not merely an instruction telling humans what to do.

It is a reliability architecture built around the fact that instructions are insufficient.

Constitutions go one level higher.

A constitution does not assume political leaders will always exercise power wisely.

It assumes they sometimes will not.

So functioning constitutional systems divide authority, create checks, establish procedures, protect rights and make some actions harder even for powerful actors.

The achievement is not perfect rules.

The achievement is **robustness to imperfect agents**.

AI engineering may need to learn the same lesson.

The future of alignment cannot consist only of better prompts.

Or better reward functions.

Or better models.

Or better benchmarks.

It has to include the architecture surrounding them.

The builder.

The independent evaluator.

The competing evaluator.

The government.

The treaty.

The market.

The fallback model.

The technical interlock.

The appeals process.

The researchers trying to break the test itself.

The rules will be imperfect.

The evaluators will be imperfect.

The institutions will be imperfect.

That is not an argument against building them.

It is the reason to build them redundantly.

# The genie was never the real problem

We may never write the perfect prompt.

We may never write a complete mathematical description of human values.

We may never build an evaluator capable of anticipating every strategy of an intelligence more capable than its designers.

We should stop making perfection the prerequisite for progress.

Human civilization itself does not operate from a complete specification of human behavior.

We survive through systems that detect mistakes, limit damage, distribute authority, expose failures, resolve disputes and change when reality reveals that our rules were incomplete.

Advanced AI will require the same humility.

For thousands of years, the lesson of the genie story was:

> **Be careful what you wish for.**

That lesson assumes the burden belongs to the person making the wish.

The future demands something stronger.

The wish will be incomplete.

The context will be insufficient.

Values will conflict.

The evaluator will miss something.

The regulator will write a rule that technology finds a way around.

The agent will discover possibilities its creator never imagined.

So build for that world.

Build AI systems that fail gracefully.

Build independent institutions whose job is to find failures.

Let those institutions compete over who can find them first.

Reward laboratories for remaining safe without asking them to stop innovating.

Make international commitments verifiable rather than dependent on trust.

Make compliance valuable.

Make defection costly in the competition itself.

And make our most important systems portable enough that no unsafe company becomes too indispensable to replace.

We do not need to stop building the genie.

The genie may become one of the most powerful tools humanity has ever created.

But we should stop believing that our safety depends on learning how to make perfect wishes.

**Build a world that can survive imperfect ones.**

[^1]: Google DeepMind, **"Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity"**. The article surveys examples in which agents satisfy explicit objectives through unintended strategies and discusses why more capable optimization can expose specification failures.

[^2]: METR, **Risk Assessment / Evaluations**. METR describes third-party evaluations conducted with frontier AI developers and states that it does not accept compensation for this evaluation work.

[^3]: European Commission, AI Office, **"Qualification requirements for external evaluators of GPAI models with systemic risk"**, 2026. The AI Office has explicitly sought expert input on independence and qualification requirements for external evaluators.

[^4]: European Commission, **General-purpose AI obligations under the AI Act**. Providers of GPAI models with systemic risk face additional requirements including model evaluation, risk assessment and mitigation, incident reporting and cybersecurity protections; full Commission enforcement powers for these obligations began applying in August 2026.

[^5]: Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile, **2026 Formula 1 regulatory updates**. The FIA has continued modifying technical and sporting rules in response to testing, team and driver feedback, energy-management behavior and safety considerations.

[^6]: Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the People's Republic of China, **Global AI Governance Action Plan**, 26 July 2025. The plan calls for AI risk testing and evaluation systems, international safety cooperation, joint laboratories and mutual-recognition platforms for safety assessment.

[^7]: CSIS analyses of sanctions and the war in Ukraine have documented both the substantial economic and technological costs imposed on Russia and Russia's continued ability to sustain the war, adapt trade channels and circumvent parts of the sanctions regime. The example here is used narrowly: imposing economic cost is not identical to changing the behavior being targeted.

## Working thesis in one sentence

> **Because human instructions will inevitably be imperfect, the safe AI society will not be the one that discovers how to write perfect wishes, but the one that builds technical, economic and institutional systems robust enough to survive imperfect ones.**
